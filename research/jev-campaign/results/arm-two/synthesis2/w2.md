## Summary

The packet covers two distinct restriction regimes: the Manifest V2 end-of-life timeline, and the quantitative limits of the declarativeNetRequest (DNR) and webRequest APIs. On both, some limits were raised and the raise stuck, while other limits survived the same change untouched. The MV2 sources are a superseded pair: a March 2025 statement that enterprises are exempt "until at least June 2025" and that users can still re-enable MV2 is overtaken by a July 2025 statement that on Chrome 138 users can no longer re-enable, and that the ExtensionManifestV2Availability policy is removed in Chrome 139. On DNR, Chrome 120 raised the simultaneously-enabled static ruleset count from 10 to 50 (of 100 total), and Chrome 121 raised the dynamic-rule ceiling from 5,000 to 30,000 — but only for rules with a "safe" action (block, allow, allowAllRequests, upgradeScheme). Everything else stayed at 5,000, and two limits are untouched by either change: 1,000 regex rules per type and 2KB per compiled rule. The enterprise policy exemption is the clearest scope-bounded exception in the packet, and it expires; the blocking-webRequest exemption for policy-installed extensions is the other, and it does not.

## Findings

1. **(05158f86 + 7c82d4f5, combined.)** The Chrome 120 increase described prospectively in the November 2023 blog post ("up to 50 to be enabled simultaneously... 100 rulesets in total", shipping in Chrome 120) is confirmed as landed by the June 2025 API reference, which states 100 static rulesets declarable in "rule_resources" and 50 enabled at a time (MAX_NUMBER_OF_ENABLED_STATIC_RULESETS). The pre-change figures — 50 offered, 10 enabled — survive only in the blog post as history. Someone would design for 50 concurrently enabled lists and would not cite the blog post's "50 lists / 10 enabled" sentence as a current limit.

2. **(2741637b + a65606cd, combined.)** The Chrome 121 dynamic-rule increase to 30,000 is conditional on action type: both sources restrict it to "safe" rules, defined in 2741637b as action block, allow, allowAllRequests or upgradeScheme, exposed as MAX_NUMBER_OF_DYNAMIC_RULES. 2741637b states the limit for all other dynamic rules "stays at 5,000"; a65606cd names that unsafe floor MAX_NUMBER_OF_UNSAFE_DYNAMIC_RULES and adds that unsafe rules added within the 5,000 allowance also count against the 30,000. Someone would budget redirect/modifyHeaders rules against 5,000, not 30,000, and would subtract those from the safe-rule headroom.

3. **(7c82d4f5.)** The 1,000-rule regex cap (MAX_NUMBER_OF_REGEX_RULES) applies per rule type and is stated in a June 2025 reference, i.e. it survived both the Chrome 120 and Chrome 121 increases unchanged; the same source adds that each rule must be under 2KB compiled. Someone would treat regex capacity as a hard, separate budget rather than assuming it scales with the raised static or dynamic totals.

4. **(7c82d4f5.)** Static rulesets are collectively guaranteed *at least* 30,000 rules (GUARANTEED_MINIMUM_STATIC_RULES) — a floor, not a cap, and a different quantity from the dynamic 30,000 in 2741637b/a65606cd despite the coincident number. Someone would avoid conflating the static guarantee with the dynamic safe-rule ceiling.

5. **(a65606cd.)** Session rules are capped at 5,000 (MAX_NUMBER_OF_SESSION_RULES), and the pre-Chrome-120 arrangement of a single 5,000 limit *combined* across dynamic and session rules no longer applies — the source states this twice. Someone targeting Chrome 120+ would size dynamic and session budgets independently.

6. **(688c66f7.)** Under Manifest V3, blocking webRequest handlers are not removed outright: the "webRequestBlocking" permission is "only available to policy installed extensions", and registering such a handler requires both "webRequest" and "webRequestBlocking". The exception is scoped to installation channel, not to extension identity or use case. Someone shipping to the Chrome Web Store would use declarativeNetRequest; someone deploying by enterprise policy could still use blocking webRequest.

7. **(095a5599 superseded by 79250f1d.)** The March 2025 source is no longer current in two respects the July 2025 source contradicts: it says users "continue to be able to turn their Manifest V2 extensions back on" (79250f1d: as of Chrome 138, "Users can no longer turn them back on"), and it bounds the enterprise exemption only as "until at least June 2025" (79250f1d: the policy "will be removed with Chrome 139"). Both agree Chrome 138 is the last version on which MV2 works and that the Chrome 139 change hits all users at once. Someone answering from 095a5599 alone would wrongly tell a user they can re-enable MV2 on a current Chrome.

8. **(095a5599 + 79250f1d, combined.)** The enterprise policy exemption is the packet's one example of an exception that is both scope-bounded (only fleets setting ExtensionManifestV2Availability) and time-bounded (removed at Chrome 139), and unlike the consumer rollout it is not gradual — 79250f1d says the change "will affect all users on Chrome 139 simultaneously". An enterprise would treat the 138→139 upgrade as a hard cutover date rather than a staged deprecation with a tail.

## What you could not determine

- **Remotely hosted code.** The packet contains nothing on whether MV3 extensions may fetch and execute code from a developer's server, or what alternatives exist.
- **Review timelines.** Nothing in the packet addresses Chrome Web Store review duration, expedited review for rule-file-only updates, or what factors affect it.
- **Whether 79250f1d changes the pairing condition.** 095a5599 says Chrome 138 is the final MV2-supporting version "when paired with the ExtensionManifestV2Availability key"; 79250f1d says on Chrome 138 all users have MV2 disabled and cannot re-enable. The packet does not state explicitly whether a policy-managed fleet on 138 is still exempt from that disabling, though the policy's survival until its removal in 139 implies it.
- **Post-June-2025 status of the "until at least June 2025" wording.** Whether the exemption was formally extended or simply restated as ending at 139 is not settled by the text.
- **Exact Chrome version for the regex and 2KB limits' introduction.** 7c82d4f5 states them as current but gives no version history.
- **Whether the WECG's requested limit of 20 (05158f86) has any normative force.** It is mentioned only as a comparison point.
- **Session-rule action safety.** Whether the safe/unsafe distinction of 2741637b applies to session rules is not addressed.

## Frozen question set

**Q1.** On Chrome 138 with ExtensionManifestV2Availability set, their MV2 extensions still run — 095a5599 names 138 as "the final version of Chrome to support Manifest V2 extensions (when paired with the ExtensionManifestV2Availability key)". On moving to Chrome 139 the policy is removed and MV2 extensions cease to function, for all users on 139 simultaneously rather than by gradual rollout. Sources: 095a5599, 79250f1d.

**Q2.** No. Redirect is not one of the "safe" actions (block, allow, allowAllRequests, upgradeScheme) that the Chrome 121 increase to 30,000 covers, so redirect rules fall under the unsafe dynamic ceiling of 5,000 (MAX_NUMBER_OF_UNSAFE_DYNAMIC_RULES) — and any unsafe rules used also count against the 30,000 safe limit. Sources: 2741637b, a65606cd.

**Q3.** No. The total number of regular expression rules of each type cannot exceed 1,000 (MAX_NUMBER_OF_REGEX_RULES), and additionally each rule must be less than 2KB once compiled. Source: 7c82d4f5.

**Q4.** Yes, but only if it is a policy-installed extension: as of Manifest V3 the "webRequestBlocking" permission is available only to policy installed extensions, and registering a blocking handler requires both "webRequest" and "webRequestBlocking" in the manifest. Source: 688c66f7.
